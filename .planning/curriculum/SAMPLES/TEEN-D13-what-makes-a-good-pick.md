## STEP 2 — Explainer · Most people do this backwards

`{ "id": "t-d13-x1", "type": "explainer", "skill": "thesis_building" }`

**Heading:** Most people do this completely backwards

> Ask somebody why they own a stock and you'll usually get one of two answers. *"It's been going up."* Or *"it's a good company."*
>
> Both of those sound like reasons. Neither one is.
>
> "It's been going up" is a fact about the past. It tells you nothing about what you'd be buying today. And "it's a good company" is only half a sentence. A great company at a stupid price is a bad buy. A boring company at a cheap price can be a great one. **The company and the price are two different questions and you have to answer both.**
>
> But here's the thing almost nobody does: decide what order to ask their questions in.
>
> Sounds like a detail. It's not. Ask them in the wrong order and you'll spend an hour building a spreadsheet for a company you were never going to understand. Or you'll fall in love with a business before you look at what it costs — and then you'll talk yourself into the price, because you already decided.
>
> So: four questions. Fixed order. Any one of them can stop you before you waste time on the next.

## STEP 3 — Put them in order

`{ "id": "t-d13-order", "type": "order_sequence", "skill": "thesis_building" }`

**[INTERACTION: NEW — `order_sequence`. Four cards, dragged into order. On Check, wrong cards slide to their real spot and each one's reason prints underneath. This is the whole shape of Phase 2, so you build it instead of reading it.]**

> **Put these four in the order a pro actually asks them.**

Cards, shown shuffled:

| Card | Real spot | Reason (shown on reveal) |
|---|---:|---|
| **THE PRICE** — what am I paying for what I'm getting? | 3 | You can't judge a price until you know what you're buying. On its own, a price means nothing. |
| **THE BUSINESS** — how does it make money, is it growing, does it keep any of it? | 1 | If you can't explain how the money gets to the company, nothing after this is knowable. Stop here. |
| **THE THESIS** — your reason, written down: why this one, why now, and what would prove me wrong? | 4 | Only worth writing once the first three pass. Write it earlier and it's just an opinion you're about to defend. |
| **THE MOAT** — why can't some other company just copy this? | 2 | No moat means today's profits are borrowed. Somebody's coming to take them back. |

**Reinforce (right first try):**
> That order isn't a preference. It's a filter. Most names die at question one — you can't say who pays them and what for — and you just saved yourself an hour.

**If you get it wrong:**
> Two mix-ups catch everybody. Putting **price** first (you can't judge $60 a share until you know what you're buying — $60 by itself tells you literally nothing). And putting **the thesis** first (a reason written before you check anything is just an opinion you're about to spend an hour defending).

## STEP 4 — Explainer · What each question is really asking

`{ "id": "t-d13-x2", "type": "explainer", "skill": "thesis_building" }`

**Heading:** What each one actually means

> **1 · THE BUSINESS.** Can you finish this sentence without googling anything: *"____ pays ____ for ____."* For Spotify: *"Listeners pay Spotify every month to stream music, and advertisers pay to run ads on the free version."* That's how the money gets there. Then two follow-ups. Is that money growing? And does the company keep any of it after paying to run the thing? Plenty of companies grow sales for years and keep nothing.
>
> **2 · THE MOAT.** Say a rival with plenty of cash decided tomorrow to come take this business. What stops them? Sometimes it's a brand people are weirdly attached to. Sometimes it's that everyone's already there, so nobody wants to be the first to leave. Sometimes they can just make it cheaper than anybody else. Sometimes switching is a hassle — and if you've ever tried to move your playlists over, you know that one. And sometimes the honest answer is **nothing stops them**. In which case this year's profits are on loan.
>
> **3 · THE PRICE.** Now — and only now — you look at the cost. Not the share price by itself. That number's meaningless, and Day 20 gets into why. What you want is how much you're paying for each dollar the company earns, compared to what this same company normally costs and what its rivals cost. A great company can absolutely be a bad buy if everybody already agrees it's great.
>
> **4 · THE THESIS.** Your thesis is just your reason, written down so you can check it later. One sentence for why this company. One for why now. And one for what would tell you you were wrong. That last one is the one everybody skips, and it's the one that saves you. **If you can't be wrong, it's not a reason.**

## STEP 5 — Which question does this belong to?

`{ "id": "t-d13-sort", "type": "sort_buckets", "skill": "thesis_building" }`

**[INTERACTION: NEW — `sort_buckets`. Four buckets, eight cards, tap-a-card-then-tap-a-bucket. Wrong ones stay put until you hit Check, so you're grading your whole thinking, not one guess. On Check, right ones lock with a tick; wrong ones pop back with their reason, one at a time.]**

**Buckets:** `THE BUSINESS` · `THE MOAT` · `THE PRICE` · `THE THESIS`

| Card | Bucket | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| "Subscribers grew 14% last year" | THE BUSINESS | More people paying. That's question one. |
| "Moving your playlists somewhere else is a pain" | THE MOAT | Switching hassle. That's what's stopping a rival. |
| "It costs 22× what it earns; it's normally 30×" | THE PRICE | Comparing what you pay to what this company usually costs. |
| "I think the ad version is about to become the bigger business" | THE THESIS | A claim about the future, with a rough date on it. |
| "It keeps 27 cents of every dollar and that hasn't moved in three years" | THE BUSINESS | What it keeps after paying to make the thing. |
| "Its two biggest rivals are way bigger and can undercut it" | THE MOAT | "There is no moat" is still a moat answer. |
| "I'd be wrong if subscriber growth drops under 8%" | THE THESIS | That's the "prove me wrong" line. Always part of your thesis. |
| "Its rival costs 14× and grows faster" | THE PRICE | Comparing to a rival. Still a price question. |

**Reinforce:**
> Notice "its two biggest rivals can undercut it." That's a moat answer even though the answer is *there isn't one*. A question you asked and answered "no" to is finished. A question you never asked is a hole.

**If you get it wrong:**
> The sneaky one is *"I'd be wrong if subscriber growth drops under 8%."* It's got a number in it, so it feels like a business fact. It's not — it's your "prove me wrong" line, and that belongs to the thesis. Stuff you look up goes in questions 1–3. Stuff you're claiming goes in question 4.

## STEP 6 — The trap

`{ "id": "t-d13-mc1", "type": "multiple_choice", "skill": "valuation" }`

**[INTERACTION: multiple_choice — the trap question. Plenty of adults get this wrong, and the feedback says so, because it's true.]**

> **You run the checklist. Question 1: you totally get how it makes money, and it's growing fast. Question 2: it's got a real, obvious moat. It's the best business on your whole list. What now?**

- A. Stick it at the top of the list — that's what a good pick looks like
- B. Buy a little bit now and finish the checklist later
- C. Go to question 3 and find out what it costs ✓
- D. Skip to the thesis — the business speaks for itself

**Right:**
> Yes — and this is where most people faceplant, including plenty of adults. "Great company" and "good buy" are different sentences. If everybody already knows it's great, that's baked into the price, and you'd be paying today for growth that hasn't happened yet. **A great company can be a bad buy. A boring company at a low price can be a great one.** The price question isn't a formality at the end. It's the thing that turns a company into a pick.

**Wrong (A) → [KAI]:**
> *"This is the single most common mistake in investing, and it gets people with way more experience than you. Everything you've figured out so far is about the company. None of it is about what you'd pay. Two thirds of a checklist isn't a checklist — go to question three."*

**Wrong (B) →**
> *"Buy first and the rest of your research happens with your money already in. At that point you'll be hunting for reasons you were right instead of reasons you were wrong. Finish first."*

**Wrong (D) →**
> *"A thesis without the price in it is missing half the argument. 'Why now' almost always has something to do with what it costs right now."*

## STEP 8 — Why the thesis goes last

`{ "id": "t-d13-mc2", "type": "multiple_choice", "skill": "thesis_building", "framing": "last" }`

**[INTERACTION: multiple_choice with `framing: "last"` — the drawn mark loops the word the question turns on.]**

> **Why does the thesis go ~~last~~ instead of first?**

- A. It's the hardest one, so you save it for when you're warmed up
- B. Write it first and it becomes something you defend instead of something you test ✓
- C. You need the price before you can work out your return
- D. Pros always write stuff down at the end

**Right:**
> That's it. Write "Celsius is going to be huge" on day one and every fact you meet afterwards gets sorted into *supports my idea* and *ignore that*. Do the first three questions first and the thesis writes itself out of what you actually found. Which also means it might *not* write itself — and that's the outcome that saves you money.

**Wrong (C) → [KAI]:**
> *"Careful — a thesis isn't a prediction of how much you'll make. If yours has a target price in it, you wrote the wrong thing. A thesis says what has to keep being true for this to work. The reason it goes last is about your own brain, not about math. One more go."*

**Wrong (A) →**
> *"It's honestly not the hardest — question one kills more names than the other three put together. The order is about not deciding before you've looked."*
