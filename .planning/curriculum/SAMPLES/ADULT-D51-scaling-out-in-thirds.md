## STEP 2 — Explainer · You're never going to sell the top

`{ "id": "a-d51-x1", "type": "explainer", "skill": "market_psychology" }`

**Heading:** You're never going to sell at the top

> Let's get one thing out of the way. You're never going to sell at the exact top. Nobody does. Not the pros, not the guy on YouTube, not you.
>
> And chasing it costs people more money than almost any other mistake. Here's why. The top only looks like the top afterwards. While it's happening, it just looks like another good day. By the time you're sure, it's gone.
>
> So change the question. Stop asking "what's the perfect price to sell at?" — there's no answer. Start asking "what's my plan if this goes up, down, or sideways?" That one has an answer.
>
> Scaling out is that plan. You decide ahead of time that you'll sell a piece now, a piece later, and let the last piece ride.
>
> Here's the deal you're making. You give up the perfect exit. In return, you never get the worst one. You'll never be the person who watched a 60% gain turn into a loss. And you'll never be the person who dumped the whole thing a week before it doubled.
>
> That's the trade. The rest of this lesson shows you exactly how it works.

## STEP 3 — Do the math · what selling one third actually gets you

`{ "id": "a-d51-calc1", "type": "calc_step", "skill": "portfolio_construction" }`

**[INTERACTION: NEW — `calc_step`. Number pad, tolerance ±1. The facts stay pinned above the pad, so this tests whether you know what to do — not whether you remembered the numbers. Get it wrong and the working shows up one line at a time, when you tap.]**

**What you know (pinned to the screen):**
- You own **33 shares**
- You paid **$40.00** each
- So the whole thing cost you **$1,320**
- You sell **11 shares** (one third) at **$52.00**

> **How much cash does selling that first third put back in your pocket?**

**Answer:** `572` (unit `$`, tolerance ±1)

**The working, one line per tap if you get it wrong:**
1. `11 shares × $52.00` → **$572**
2. `Those same 11 shares cost you 11 × $40.00` → **$440**
3. `$572 compared to what the WHOLE thing cost you ($1,320)` → **43%**

**Reinforce:**
> Look at line 3, because that's the bit that surprises people. You sold **a third** of your shares and got back **43% of everything you put in.** Now you're holding two thirds of the position using barely half your original money. And you didn't have to predict anything — the math just did that.

**If you get it wrong → [KAI]:**
> *"The exact number matters less than the pattern here. When something's up, selling a slice hands back more than you'd think. A third of the shares, sold up 30%, gives you 43% of your money. That gap between 33% and 43% is the whole reason this beats just crossing your fingers. Have another go."*

## STEP 4 — Explainer · The three rules

`{ "id": "a-d51-x2", "type": "explainer", "skill": "portfolio_construction" }`

**Heading:** Sell a third, sell a third, let one run

> There are three rules. They're boring on purpose — boring is exactly what you want when the market's going nuts and your heart's pounding.
>
> **One. When it hits your target, sell a third.** You picked that target back when you bought it, before the price meant anything to you emotionally. So you sell there. Whether or not today feels like a good day to sell.
>
> **Two. The second that first third is sold, move your stop up to what you paid.** This takes four seconds and it's the most important thing in the whole lesson. From this moment on, this position can't lose you money. Not "probably won't." Can't. You've already banked more than the rest of it could give back.
>
> **Three. Sell another third at a second target. Then leave the last third alone** and let your stop climb behind it. The runner isn't there to be fiddled with. It's there in case you were more right than you knew. Its only job is to be allowed to keep going.
>
> One more thing, and it's the one people break. From rule two onwards, **your stop only ever moves up.** Think of it like a ratchet — it clicks up, it never slides back. If you ever catch yourself lowering a stop to "give it room to breathe", stop and reread what you wrote down. You're not following the plan any more. You're negotiating with it.

> **[ILLUSTRATION: the ratchet object from Step 2, drawn large. A toothed vertical rail with a pawl resting in a tooth, and a hand-drawn arrow showing the pawl can climb but cannot descend — the downward direction crossed with a light ink stroke. Three price labels pinned to teeth: "entry $40 — breakeven", "$52 — first target", "$61 — second target".]**

## STEP 5 — Do the math · the floor you just built

`{ "id": "a-d51-calc2", "type": "calc_step", "skill": "risk" }`

**[INTERACTION: `calc_step`]**

**What you know (pinned to the screen):**
- Cash already banked from that first third: **$572**
- Shares you still hold: **22**
- Your stop is now at what you paid: **$40.00**
- The whole thing cost you: **$1,320**

> **Say the stop gets hit tomorrow and your last 22 shares sell at $40. Did you make money or lose money on the whole trade — and how much?**

**Answer:** `132` (unit `$`, tolerance ±1)

**The working:**
1. `22 shares × $40.00` → **$880**
2. `$880 + the $572 you already banked` → **$1,452**
3. `$1,452 − the $1,320 it cost you` → **+$132**

**Reinforce:**
> That's the **worst** thing that can happen now. Not the likely thing — the worst thing. The floor under this trade is a profit. And it became a profit the moment you sold a third and dragged your stop up. Everything from here is a question of how much you make, not whether you make anything.

**If you get it wrong:**
> Almost everyone who misses this forgets the $572. They look at the 22 shares like they're the whole trade. They're not — part of this is already done and paid for. Which, by the way, is exactly what makes the rest of it easy to hold.

## STEP 6 — Try it · the one that keeps running

`{ "id": "a-d51-ladder1", "type": "scale_ladder", "skill": "portfolio_construction" }`

**[INTERACTION: NEW — `scale_ladder`. You drag your sell points onto a price scale next to a made-up chart, set your stop, and hit Check to run it. NOT GRADED — like a prediction, this is a "watch what happens", not a test. Grading it would be teaching hindsight.]**

**What you're working with:**
> 33 shares. You paid $40. Your stop is $34. Your first target is $52, your second is $61.
> Put your sell points where you want them. Then we'll press play.

**Made-up price path A:** `40 → 46 → 52 → 58 → 61 → 70 → 82 → 95 → 88`

**What happened — three ways to play it:**

| | You end with | Profit |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Your plan** (⅓ at $52, ⅓ at $61, stop trails the rest — out at $88) | **$2,211** | **+$891** |
| Sold the lot at your first target | $1,716 | +$396 |
| Held everything, just trailed the stop | **$2,904** | **+$1,584** |

**What it means:**
> **Headline:** *This time, just holding beat you. It was always going to.*
>
> **Body:** When something runs and keeps running, every share you sold early is a share you'd have made more on. Holding the lot would've made you $1,584 instead of $891. You left $693 on the table by scaling out. No way to spin that.
>
> Sit with it for a second instead of explaining it away. If you can't handle losing to the runner sometimes, you won't stick to this plan. The next screen is why it's still the right plan.

## STEP 7 — Explainer · Never first, never last

`{ "id": "a-d51-x3", "type": "explainer", "skill": "market_psychology" }`

**Heading:** Never first. Never last.

> You just watched this plan lose. The right answer isn't to defend it. It's to ask what it's actually for.
>
> It's not for winning one trade. On any single trade, something always beats it. If the stock runs, holding beats it. If the stock turns around, selling everything beats it. **There's always a better plan than scaling out — and you only find out which one afterwards.**
>
> What scaling out does is keep you off the worst end. It comes second when things go well, and second when things go badly. Do this twenty times and the plan that's never last will beat the plan that's sometimes first and sometimes a disaster. Not because it's smart. Because it doesn't need you to be.
>
> There's a second reason, and honestly it matters more for most people. **Once you've taken money off the table, you can think straight.** What's left is riding on the market's money, with a stop above what you paid. That feels completely different from having your whole stack still at risk. And thinking straight is worth way more over twenty trades than the $693 you gave up on the last screen.

## STEP 8 — Try it again · the one that turns around

`{ "id": "a-d51-ladder2", "type": "scale_ladder", "skill": "portfolio_construction" }`

**[INTERACTION: `scale_ladder` — same setup, different made-up path. The pair is the lesson; neither screen works on its own.]**

**Same setup:** 33 shares, you paid $40, stop at $34, targets $52 and $61.

**Made-up price path B:** `40 → 47 → 52 → 55 → 51 → 44 → 38 → 33`

**What happened — same three ways:**

| | You end with | Profit / loss |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Your plan** (⅓ at $52, stop moved to $40, rest out at $40) | **$1,452** | **+$132** |
| Sold the lot at your first target | **$1,716** | **+$396** |
| Held everything on the original $34 stop | $1,122 | **−$198** |

**What it means:**
> **Headline:** *This time holding lost — and this plan is the only reason you're green.*
>
> **Body:** Same trade, same rules, but the price went up and then gave it all back. Your first third sold at $52. Your stop moved to $40. When the price came back down through it, the last 22 shares went out at what you paid, and you finished **+$132** instead of **−$198**.
>
> Now put the two screens side by side. On path A, scaling out came second — behind holding. On path B, scaling out came second — ahead of holding. **It's never won. It's also never lost.**
>
> That's not a consolation prize. Over twenty trades, that IS the game. And it's why a rule beats whatever you would've decided at 9:35 in the morning with your stomach in knots.

## STEP 9 — When NOT to do this

`{ "id": "a-d51-mc1", "type": "multiple_choice", "skill": "portfolio_construction", "framing": "shouldn't" }`

**[INTERACTION: multiple_choice with `framing: "shouldn't"` — the drawn mark loops the "shouldn't", because that's where people misread.]**

> **When ~~shouldn't~~ you scale out?**

- A. It's up 40% and you're getting nervous
- B. **Your reason for owning it just broke — the thing you said would prove you wrong actually happened** ✓
- C. It's grown so big it's throwing off your whole portfolio
- D. It hit your first target on a quiet day with no news

**Right:**
> Exactly. Scaling out is for something that's still working. If your reason for owning it is dead, selling a piece is just being wrong in slow motion — you're paying to stay in something you've already decided you shouldn't own. Broken reason means get all the way out. That's the rule you wrote on Day 50.

**Wrong (A) → [KAI]:**
> *"Actually, nervous is one of the better times to take a third off — that's exactly the pressure this whole thing exists to let out, and following a rule while you're nervous is the rule doing its job. The one time selling a piece is the wrong move is when you shouldn't own any of it. Give it another shot."*

**Wrong (C) →**
> *"Nope, trimming something that's grown too big is totally fine — that's Day 53, and it's the same move with a different reason behind it."*

**Wrong (D) →**
> *"Quiet days are the best days to do this, because there's nothing to argue with. Target's a target."*

## STEP 10 — True / false · the ratchet

`{ "id": "a-d51-tf", "type": "true_false", "skill": "risk", "trueLabel": "Makes sense", "falseLabel": "Bad idea" }`

**[INTERACTION: true_false, relabelled Makes sense / Bad idea, rendered as the swipe]**

> **"My last third has dipped back near my raised stop. I'll drop the stop a couple of bucks to give it room to breathe."**

**Answer: Bad idea.**

**If you swipe wrong:**
> The second you lower a stop, it's not a stop any more. It's a thing you move whenever it's about to do its job. Which is the same as not having one.
>
> And listen to the reason underneath it. "Give it room to breathe" isn't a fact about the company. It's you not wanting to get stopped out. That's the exact feeling the ratchet exists to overrule. If the level was wrong, it was wrong when you set it — calmly, with the chart in front of you. Fix how you pick it next time. Don't fix the number this time.

**If you swipe right:**
> Yep. The stop goes up or it stays put. Nobody in history has ever improved a stop by moving it down.

## STEP 11 — Go do it for real

`{ "id": "a-d51-rw", "type": "real_world", "action": "paper_trade", "skill": "portfolio_construction" }`

**[INTERACTION: `real_world` with the NEW `paper_trade` action. Checked by a real paper sell order for part of an existing paper position, plus a stop change on the rest. Today the rep IS the skill — nothing else would teach it.]**

**Prompt:**
> Open your paper account. Find whatever's up the most. Sell **one third of it.**
>
> Then move the stop on what's left up to what you paid.
>
> Do both. The second one takes four seconds and it's the part that matters.

**CTA:** `Open my paper account`

**Success text:**
> That position can't lose you money any more, and you've got a runner going. You just did the thing most investors talk about and never actually do — and you did it with fake money, which is the only sensible time to learn it.

**If nothing's up yet:**
> Nothing green right now? That's normal, and it's fine. Write the plan instead: your first target, your second target, and the words "move stop to what I paid" next to the first one. Stick it on your biggest position. It'll be there when the price is.
