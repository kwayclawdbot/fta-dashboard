The Foundation

The doorway to the market

A brokerage account is an account with a licensed firm that lets you buy and sell securities — stocks, funds, and later, options.

You can't walk up to an exchange and buy a share yourself. The broker is the licensed doorway, and the account is your key. Opening one is the practical first step of your investing life.
Cash in, orders out, holdings back. That loop is the whole machine.
Bank account

Holds cash

Built for spending and saving. Your money sits as dollars, available but flat.

Brokerage account

Holds investments

Built for owning. Your money becomes shares and funds that can grow over time.

Choosing a Broker

Compare three things, then check the gate

The stock is the same wherever you buy it. What differs is what surrounds the trade: costs, tools, and support.

Run the same checklist on every broker: costs, tools, support.

Costs are commissions and fees. Tools are the charts, research, and order screens you'll live in. Support is whether a real human answers when something goes wrong. Also check it offers the account types you want.

Options approval levels gate strategies by experience.

Ask a broker for options trading and it won't just switch on. You answer questions about your experience and goals, and the broker assigns an approval level. Lower rungs unlock the basic strategies; riskier ones stay locked until you've earned the higher rungs. The ladder matches strategy risk to your experience, and it protects beginners from tools they aren't ready for.
Everyone starts low on the ladder. That's normal, and it's temporary. You climb by learning and by trading responsibly.
Account Types

Five jars, five different jobs

Same dollars, different wrappers. The wrapper decides how your money is taxed and what rules it lives under.

Pick the jar by the goal, not the other way around.

A taxable brokerage account is the open jar: no special tax breaks, no special rules. Invest any amount, sell and withdraw whenever. Maximum flexibility.
Traditional IRA

Tax break now

Contributions can be deducted from your taxable income today. You pay tax later, when you withdraw in retirement.

Roth IRA

Tax-free later

You contribute money you've already paid tax on. Qualified withdrawals in retirement come out tax-free.

401(k)

The workplace jar

A retirement plan that lives at your job. Many employers match part of what you contribute. That match is extra pay — take it.

529 plan

The education jar

Built for education costs, with tax advantages when the money is used for qualified education expenses.

One honest note: contribution limits and tax rules change year to year, so verify the current figures before you fund an account. The concepts here don't change; the numbers do.

Lock It In

Quick check

Five questions. Answer them to earn your points for the lesson.

1. What is a brokerage account?

A brokerage account is your doorway to the market: an account with a licensed firm through which you buy and sell securities like stocks and funds.

2. Which statement best describes a Roth IRA?

The Roth flips the tax order: you pay tax on the money first, contribute it, and qualified withdrawals in retirement come out tax-free. The deduct-now version is the Traditional IRA.

3. Why do brokers use options approval levels?

Approval levels are a safety ladder. Riskier strategies sit on higher rungs, and the broker matches your level to your experience and goals so beginners aren't handed tools they're not ready for.

4. What is the smart way to choose a broker?

The stock is the same everywhere; the experience isn't. Run the same checklist on each broker: costs, tools and usability, support, and whether it offers the account types you need.

5. Which of these is a common benefit of a 401(k)?

Many employers match part of what you contribute to a 401(k). That match is extra compensation on top of your salary, which is why the standing advice is to capture it.
