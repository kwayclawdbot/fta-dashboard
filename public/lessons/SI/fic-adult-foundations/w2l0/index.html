How to Read a Business (Before You Read a Chart)

A chart shows you what a price did. It never tells you what a company is. Before the candles, learn the five questions that decode any business in plain English, and you will pick stocks to study for a reason, not a hunch.

SI-100 · Adults ~5 min 5 questions, one method
Read it once and you own the method.
Start Here

A business is a machine

A business takes something people want and turns it into money. That is the whole idea, whether the company sells shoes, software, or coffee.

Three plain words run every one of them. Revenue is the money coming in when people buy. Costs are what it takes to run the machine: the product, the workers, the buildings. Profit is what is left when you take costs out of revenue. Revenue minus costs equals profit. A giant company and a lemonade stand run on that exact sentence.

Here is why this comes before charts. A candlestick tells you what a price did last Tuesday. It cannot tell you whether the machine behind the price is any good. Judge the business first, and the chart becomes a tool instead of a guess.

The Method

Five questions decode any company

Ask these in order, in plain English. No jargon, no metaphors yet. If you can answer all five, you understand the business.

1

What do they sell?

Name the actual product or service in one sentence. If you cannot say it plainly, you do not understand it yet.

2

How do they make money?

Follow the dollar. Sometimes the thing they sell is not where the profit is. That gap is often the whole story.

3

Why do customers keep coming back?

What makes people choose this one again, and would be hard for a rival to copy? This is the difference between a good year and a good business.

4

What could make people buy less?

Every business has a weak spot. Say it out loud. Studying the risk is not pessimism; it is the job.

5

Is the machine getting stronger or weaker?

Pick the one or two numbers that matter, and ask whether they are improving over time. Direction beats any single snapshot.

Worked Example

Run Costco through the five

One recognizable company, five plain answers. Notice how question two changes everything.

1 · What do they sell?
Groceries, household goods, and just about everything else, in bulk, inside giant warehouse stores you have to be a member to enter.
2 · How do they make money?
Here is the surprise. Costco barely profits on the groceries; it sells them at close to what it pays. The real profit is the yearly membership fee millions of families pay just to walk in the door.
3 · Why do customers come back?
Prices are low because Costco buys in enormous quantities and passes the savings on. Members have already paid to belong, so they keep using it. That loyalty is the moat.
4 · What could make people buy less?
A cheaper option appears, or members decide the fee is not worth it and do not renew. Thin margins also mean a jump in the cost of goods can squeeze the whole model.
5 · Stronger or weaker?
Watch two numbers: the renewal rate and new sign-ups. Rising renewals mean the machine is getting stronger, no matter what the price did this week.

Now the picture, since you have the method. Reading a business is sizing up a lemonade stand before you buy a share of it. You would want to know what it sells, where the money really comes from, why the same kids come back every day, what could dry up the line, and whether more customers show up each week than last. The five questions are that, scaled to any company on earth.

This is the exact method behind the Company of the Week. Every week in the Club you will run one real company through these same five questions with your family. Do it enough times and you will read a business faster than most people read a headline, and the charts in the next lessons will finally have something to point at.

Lock It In

Quick check

Five questions. Answer them to earn your points and prove you own the method.

1. Before you ever open its chart, what should you understand about a company first?

The business comes first. A chart shows what a price did; the five questions tell you whether the machine behind it is any good.

2. Costco barely profits on its groceries. Where does most of its profit come from?

Question two often changes the whole story. The cheap groceries get members in the door; the membership fee is the real engine.

3. "Why do customers keep coming back?" is really asking about what?

A durable reason to return is the difference between a good year and a good business.

4. Which of the five questions is the one that surfaces the risk?

Naming the weak spot out loud is part of the job, not pessimism. Every business has one.

5. To judge whether a business is getting stronger, you mainly look at whether...

Direction over time beats any single snapshot. Pick the one or two numbers that matter and watch the trend.
