Three Ways to Fill

Market, limit, stop

Every order starts with one decision: how should this fill? Three tickets, three different promises.

An order type decides what is guaranteed — the speed of the fill, or the price you pay.

You never get both. Market guarantees the fill. Limit guarantees the price. A stop stands guard and acts only when its price is hit.
Market order

Fills now

Executes right away at the best current price. Speed is guaranteed; the exact price is not.

Limit order

Your price or better

Fills only at your price or better. Price is guaranteed; the fill is not — it may simply wait.

Stop order

Wakes up at your line

Sleeps until the stop price is hit, then becomes a market order. Your automatic exit.

Illustrative: the fifty, forty-nine, and forty-seven dollar levels are a worked example — the shape of the idea, not a forecast or a recommendation.
Two Prices, One Gap

The bid-ask spread

Before any order fills, a buyer's best offer and a seller's lowest price stare at each other. The gap between them is the spread.

The bid is the highest price a buyer offers. The ask is the lowest a seller accepts.

Bid $49.95, ask $50.05 — a ten-cent spread. Illustrative numbers, but the reading is real: the smaller that gap, the closer buyers and sellers agree.
Narrow spread

Liquid market

Plenty of buyers and sellers packed close together. Your order fills near the price on the screen.

Wide spread

Thin market

Fewer players, prices far apart. Fills drift away from what you expected, and that drift costs money.

Glance at the spread before you trade. It tells you how crowded the room is before you walk in.

Lock It In

Last quiz of the course

Five questions. Answer them to earn your points for the lesson.

1. You tap buy with a market order. What actually happens?

A market order trades price control for speed. It fills now, at whatever the best available price is at that moment, which may be slightly different from the last price you saw.

2. What does a limit order guarantee, and what doesn't it?

A limit order controls price: your price or better, never worse. The tradeoff is certainty. If the stock never reaches your limit, the order simply waits unfilled.

3. A stop order sits below the current price. What happens when the stock falls to the stop price?

A stop order sleeps until the stop price trades. The moment it's hit, it wakes up as a market order, the automatic exit that caps a loss before a slide gets worse.

4. What is the bid-ask spread?

The bid is the highest price buyers offer; the ask is the lowest sellers accept. The gap between them is the spread, and a narrow spread signals a liquid market.

5. Your checklist is complete: thesis, entry, stop, size, exit, order type. What's the smart next step?

Practice with paper trading before the first real trade. Rehearse the full checklist on a simulator until it feels boring, so real money only ever follows a rehearsed plan.
