The Company Behind the Ticker

Behind every ticker is a real business with real products, customers, and revenue. Fundamental analysis asks whether it is actually a good one. In the next few minutes you'll learn to read a company the way an owner would.

Two Ways to Look

Read the chart, or read the company

Two toolkits. Different questions.

Technical

Reads the chart

Price, patterns, and volume. It studies what buyers and sellers are doing right now, and where they might act next.

Fundamental

Reads the company

Financial statements, industry, and the wider economy. It estimates a company's intrinsic value and how fast it can grow.

Technical analysis asks "what is price doing?" Fundamental analysis asks "what is this business worth?" Strong investors learn to use both.
The Reports

Three financial statements

Every public company reports its health three ways. Each one answers a different question about the business.

One shows profit. One shows position. One shows cash.

Read together, the three statements tell you whether a company earns money, what it owns and owes, and where its cash really goes.
Over a period

Income statement

Revenue minus expenses gives net income. It tells you whether the company actually made money during the period.

A point in time

Balance sheet

A snapshot of assets on one side, liabilities and equity on the other. What the company owns and what it owes.

Follow the cash

Cash flow statement

Tracks real cash split into operating, investing, and financing activities. Profit on paper is not the same as cash in hand.

The Key Numbers

P/E, EPS, and a health check

Two ratios tell you profit and price. EPS and P/E.

EPS

Profit per share

Earnings per share is the company's profit divided across its shares. It is the slice of profit attributed to each share you own.

P/E

Price per dollar of earnings

Share price divided by EPS. It shows how much the market pays for each dollar of earnings. Only meaningful compared to peers.

Two more health checks. The current ratio measures short-term liquidity, whether a company can cover its near-term bills. Debt-to-equity measures leverage, how much it borrows against what owners put in.
Stocks move on catalysts, like earnings reports and major news. Companies file a 10-K each year, a 10-Q each quarter, and an 8-K when a major event happens. Watching the calendar for these is part of the job.

Lock It In

Quick check

Five questions. Answer them to earn your points for the lesson.

1. What is the primary purpose of fundamental analysis?

Fundamental analysis evaluates a company's financials, industry, and economics to estimate intrinsic value.

2. What does the price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio measure?

P/E compares share price to earnings per share, showing how the market values each dollar of earnings.

3. Which financial statement reports cash flows from operating, investing, and financing activities?

The cash flow statement reports cash from operating, investing, and financing activities.

4. What do liquidity ratios like the current ratio measure?

Liquidity ratios such as the current ratio measure the ability to cover short-term obligations.

5. What is EPS (earnings per share)?

EPS is the profit attributed to each outstanding share, a core profitability measure.
