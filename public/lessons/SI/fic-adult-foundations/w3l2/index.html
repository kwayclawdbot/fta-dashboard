Two Ways to Look

Read the chart, or read the company

Two toolkits, two different questions. Technical analysis studies price. Fundamental analysis studies the business behind it. Strong investors learn to use both.

Technical

Reads the chart

Price, patterns, and volume. It studies what buyers and sellers are doing right now, and where they might act next.

Asks: “What is price doing?”
vs
Fundamental

Reads the company

Financial statements, the industry, and the wider economy. It estimates what a company is truly worth and how fast it can grow.

Asks: “What is this business worth?”
Same stock, two lenses. Technical asks when to act. Fundamental asks whether the business deserves your money in the first place. This lesson lives on the fundamental side.
The Reports

Three financial statements

Every public company reports its health three ways. Each one answers a different question about the business.

Over a period

Income statement

Revenue minus expenses gives net income. It tells you whether the company actually made money during the period.

A point in time

Balance sheet

A snapshot of assets on one side, liabilities and equity on the other. What the company owns and what it owes.

Follow the cash

Cash flow statement

Real cash split into operating, investing, and financing. Profit on paper is not the same as cash in hand.

The Key Numbers

P/E, EPS, and what moves a stock

Two ratios turn a giant report into something you can compare in seconds. Then catalysts decide when the market re-prices what you found.

EPS

Profit per share

Earnings per share is the company's profit divided across its shares. It's the slice of profit attributed to each share you own.

P/E

Price per dollar of earnings

Share price divided by EPS. It shows how much the market pays for each dollar of earnings — only meaningful compared to peers.

Four companies · illustrative · not recommendations
By P/E, Beta looks priciest at 40× — even with the lowest share price.
Highest price and highest valuation aren't the same thing. Always compare P/E to peers in the same industry.
Two quick health checks round it out. The current ratio asks whether a company can cover its short-term bills. Debt-to-equity asks how much it borrows against what owners put in. Stocks then move on catalysts — earnings and major news. Companies file a 10-K yearly, a 10-Q quarterly, and an 8-K when something big happens.

Lock It In

Quick check

Five questions. Answer them to earn your points and prove you can read a company.

1. What is the primary purpose of fundamental analysis?

Fundamental analysis evaluates a company's financials, industry, and economics to estimate its intrinsic value. Chart-reading is technical analysis.

2. What does the price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio measure?

P/E compares share price to earnings per share, showing how much the market pays for each dollar of earnings. It's only meaningful versus peers.

3. Which financial statement reports cash from operating, investing, and financing activities?

The cash flow statement tracks real cash split into operating, investing, and financing activities — because paper profit isn't the same as cash in hand.

4. A company files an 8-K. What does that usually signal?

An 8-K is filed when something major happens. The 10-K is yearly and the 10-Q is quarterly. These events are the catalysts that re-price a stock.

5. What is EPS (earnings per share)?

EPS is profit divided across the shares — the slice of earnings attributed to each share you own, and the denominator inside the P/E ratio.
