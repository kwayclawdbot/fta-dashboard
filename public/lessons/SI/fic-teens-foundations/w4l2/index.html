The Superpower and the Trap

Leverage cuts both ways

A little money controls a much bigger position. That is leverage. Drag the stock's move and watch what happens to your option. It amplifies both directions, hard.

When you are right

Gains multiply

A small move in the stock turns into a big move in your option. That is the amazing upside everyone chases.

When you are wrong

Losses multiply

The lever does not care which way you miss. It amplifies losses exactly as hard, which is why options move so fast.

Two Endings

Grow big, or go to zero

Watch the two paths. One option catches the move and rips higher. The other fades to nothing. When you buy an option, the premium you paid is the most you can lose — and it really can hit zero.

The Sneaky Enemy

Time decay: the melting premium

Every option has a deadline. Every day, a little of your premium quietly melts away — even when the stock does nothing. And the fewer days left, the faster it melts.

With 90 days left, one quiet day costs about $1
Premium left as the deadline nears  ·  the curve dives fastest right before expiration
Wait too long

It melts

Like an ice cube in the sun. The move can finally show up after your option has already faded to almost nothing.

The takeaway

Time is not free

The clock runs even on quiet days. You are paying rent on that contract, and the last week is the most expensive.

Lock It In

Quick check

Five questions. Show me you respect the leverage and the clock.

1. What is the most a buyer can lose on an option they bought?

When you buy an option, the premium you paid is your maximum loss if it expires worthless. It can hit zero, but not go below.

2. Why does “leverage” make options risky?

Leverage lets a small amount control a big position. That multiplies gains, but it multiplies losses exactly the same way.

3. What is “time decay”?

As the deadline approaches there is less time for the stock to move, so the option quietly loses value. Waiting costs you, and the last week costs the most.

4. You think a stock is going UP. Which option fits a bullish view?

A call is the right to buy at a set price, so it fits a bullish (up) outlook. A put fits a bearish (down) one.

5. Should a beginner rush into trading options with real money?

Options add risk and complexity and can expire worthless. Learn stocks first, practice on paper, and understand each contract before using real money.
